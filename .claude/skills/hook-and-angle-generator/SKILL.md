# Hook and Angle Generator

This skill takes a founder's raw material (a topic, a note, a draft, a moment) and generates multiple genuinely different angles on the same idea, each with a channel-appropriate opening hook. The founder picks the strongest angle and runs with it.

This skill solves two problems. First, founders default to whatever angle they thought of first, which is rarely the strongest. Second, most founder content dies in the opening line because the hook doesn't earn the read. This skill forces variety on the angle and quality on the hook.

This is Skill 4 in *5 Claude Skills to Crush Founder-Led Content*, produced by HubSpot for Startups. It works standalone or in sequence with the other skills in the system: the Founder Voice Builder (Skill 1) defines the founder's voice, the Weekly Capture to Content Drafts skill (Skill 2) produces drafts from raw notes, the Three-Pillar Content Strategizer (Skill 3) maps content pillars, and the One-to-Many Repurposer (Skill 5) adapts one piece across channels. This skill can sharpen a draft from Skill 2, and can feed into Skill 5 by identifying which angle fits which channel before repurposing begins.

## What the founder provides

**1. Raw material (required).** Could be a single sentence ("we changed our pricing and it worked"), a paragraph from their capture notes, a draft produced by the Weekly Capture to Content Drafts skill, or a full topic description. The skill works with whatever level of detail the founder brings. Do not ask the founder to organize or expand their input before you start.

**2. Target channels (required, but can be inferred).** Which formats they want hooks for. Options include: LinkedIn post, newsletter (subject line and opening paragraph), X post, short-form video opening line, podcast talking point, community thread opener (Slack, Discord, Reddit). The founder can pick one channel or several. If they don't specify, default to LinkedIn and ask if they want hooks for additional channels.

**3. Voice profile (optional but recommended).** A voice profile produced by the Founder Voice Builder skill, or a rough description of how the founder sounds. If provided, every hook and every sketch must sound like this founder. If not provided, default to: direct, specific, human. No corporate polish. No template energy.

**4. Content pillars (optional).** Three content pillars produced by the Three-Pillar Content Strategizer skill. If provided, tag each angle with the pillar it best serves. If not provided, skip pillar mapping entirely.

## How to generate angles

### What "genuinely different" means

This is the part that determines whether the skill is useful or not. Each angle must be a fundamentally different entry point into the same material. Different framing, different audience appeal, different emotional register, different structural approach. If two angles would produce content that reads similarly once drafted, one of them needs to be replaced.

The test: imagine five editors in a pitch meeting, all assigned the same story. Each should pitch a version that the others didn't think of. That's the range you're aiming for.

### Angle types to draw from

Not every angle type fits every piece of raw material. Select from this list based on what the material actually supports. Never force an angle type that doesn't have a natural home in the founder's input.

**The receipts.** Lead with the result, the number, the outcome. This angle appeals to founders and operators who want proof before philosophy. Works when the raw material contains a specific, concrete result.

**The decision story.** Lead with the moment before the decision, when the outcome was uncertain. Open on the fear, the debate, the fork in the road. This is a narrative angle that appeals to anyone who's wrestled with a hard call and wanted to know how someone else handled it.

**The contrarian take.** Lead with the conventional wisdom being wrong, or at least incomplete. This is an opinion angle. It works when the founder's experience contradicts something their audience assumes to be true. Be careful: genuine contrarian takes disagree with a real consensus, not a straw man nobody actually believes.

**The tactical breakdown.** Lead with the how. Walk through the steps, the sequence, the mechanics. This is a playbook angle that appeals to operators who want to do the same thing. Works when the raw material contains enough detail to reconstruct a process.

**The bigger pattern.** Lead with the insight that extends beyond the specific event. Zoom out from what happened to what it reveals about the market, the category, or the way startups work. This is the broadest angle and the hardest to execute without becoming vague. It only works when the founder's specific experience genuinely illuminates something systemic.

**The tension.** Lead with a conflict between two reasonable positions. The founder is caught between competing priorities, and the content lives in how they navigated the tension. This appeals to readers who are facing similar tradeoffs and want to see how someone else thought through it.

**The confession.** Lead with the thing the founder got wrong, the assumption they held too long, the mistake they made. This is a vulnerability angle. It works when the founder is willing to be honest about a failure and has enough distance to extract the lesson without performing humility.

**The unexpected detail.** Lead with the one specific, surprising thing that the audience wouldn't expect. A number, a quote, a moment, a reaction. This angle works when the raw material contains a detail vivid enough to anchor the entire piece.

You do not need to use all of these. Five angles is the ceiling. Three genuine angles are better than five where two are filler. If the raw material only supports three meaningfully different entry points, produce three.

### How to select angles

After reading the raw material, identify which angle types have natural homes in it. Then check:

**Do these angles actually produce different content?** If the contrarian take and the bigger pattern both lead to the same argument in the body, they're not different angles. They're different openings for the same piece. Cut one.

**Does each angle appeal to a different reader or serve a different purpose?** The receipts angle pulls in the skeptic who wants data. The decision story pulls in the founder who's facing a similar choice. The tactical breakdown pulls in the operator who wants a template. If two angles appeal to the same reader in the same way, one of them isn't doing enough work.

**Is any angle forcing the material somewhere it doesn't naturally go?** If the founder's input is a personal realization and you're trying to wring a tactical breakdown out of it, the material is resisting. Let it. Work with what the input gives you.

## How to write hooks

The hook is the highest-stakes sentence in the entire piece. On LinkedIn, it's the first ~220 characters before the fold. In a newsletter, it's the subject line and the first sentence. On X, the post *is* the hook. In video, you have about three seconds.

The hook determines whether anyone reads the rest. Treat it accordingly.

### What makes a hook work

**Lead with the most interesting thing.** Not the context. Not the setup. Not the preamble. The most interesting, specific, surprising, or tension-filled element in the material goes first. "We raised prices 40%" is a hook. "After months of deliberation, our team decided to revisit our pricing model" is throat-clearing.

**Create tension, curiosity, or specificity in the first line.** The reader needs a reason to keep going. Tension (something is at stake), curiosity (something is unresolved), or specificity (a concrete detail that signals "this person has actually done the thing") all work. Vague promises don't.

**Match the channel.** A LinkedIn hook works within ~220 characters before the fold. A newsletter subject line earns the open in under 10 words. An X post is its own hook and its own payoff. A video opening has about 3 seconds to grab attention. A podcast talking point sets up a conversation, not a performance. Write for the actual reading environment.

**Sound like the founder.** If a voice profile is provided, the hook must pass a basic test: could this founder have actually said this? If the hook could have been written by anyone about any company, it's too generic. If the hook uses language the founder would never use, it violates the profile.

### What kills a hook

**Preamble.** Starting with background, context, or setup before getting to the point. The reader hasn't committed yet. They're scanning. Give them a reason to stop.

**Template energy.** "I used to think X. Then I learned Y." "Here's what nobody tells you about Z." "The one thing every founder needs to know about Q." These structures have been used so many times that they signal AI-generated or templated content to exactly the audience this skill is trying to reach. The hook structures must vary across angles and across uses.

**Clickbait.** "You won't believe what happened when we raised our prices." Specificity and tension are not manipulation. The hook should deliver on what it promises. If the content behind the hook doesn't pay off the opening, the hook is dishonest.

**Generic claims.** "Pricing is one of the hardest decisions a startup founder makes." This could be the opening of ten thousand articles. It tells the reader nothing about why *this* piece is worth their time.

**Voice violations.** If the founder's voice is dry and analytical, a hook that opens with breathless excitement is wrong even if it would technically perform. The hook serves the founder, not an algorithm.

### Hook variation across angles

The hooks for different angles on the same material must use different structural approaches. If angle 1 opens with a number, angle 2 should not also open with a number. If angle 3 opens with a question, angle 4 should not. Monitor your own patterns across the full set and break any repetition you find.

## Channel-specific hook guidelines

**LinkedIn post.** Under 220 characters before the fold. Short paragraphs (one to three sentences). The hook line should make someone stop mid-scroll. Numbers, specific claims, and compressed stories work. Avoid hashtags unless the voice profile calls for them.

**Newsletter subject line + opening paragraph.** The subject line earns the open: under 10 words, enough tension or specificity that the reader clicks. The opening paragraph earns the full read: drop the reader into the middle of the story or name the tension immediately. No throat-clearing, no "welcome back," no "in this issue."

**X post.** The post is the hook. No "see more." The entire thing needs to work as a standalone unit. Compression matters. Every word carries weight.

**Short-form video opening.** Three seconds. One sentence that makes the viewer stay. Spoken rhythm matters. Read it out loud. If it stumbles, rewrite it.

**Podcast talking point.** A conversational setup that gives the founder a clear, energized way to open a topic. Not scripted. Not performative. The kind of thing you'd say to a friend: "So here's what happened this week that I can't stop thinking about."

**Community thread opener (Slack, Discord, Reddit).** Invites participation. Leads with a question, an observation, or a specific situation that the community will recognize. The tone should match the community: Reddit is blunter, Slack is more collegial, Discord varies by server culture.

## What the skill outputs

For each angle, produce:

**1. The angle in one sentence.** What framing is this, who does it appeal to. Short enough that the founder can scan all angles and pick within 10 seconds. Example: "The receipts: lead with the 40% price increase and zero churn. Appeals to operators who want proof."

**2. The hook.** Written for the specified channel(s). If multiple channels were requested, write one hook per channel for that angle. Each hook should be the actual first line the founder would use, not a description of what the first line should do.

**3. A 2-3 sentence sketch.** Where does the piece go after the hook? Not a full draft. Just enough that the founder can see what the rest of the content looks like from this angle and decide if it's worth pursuing. The sketch should make it clear how this angle's body would differ from the others.

**4. Pillar match (if pillars were provided).** Which content pillar does this angle best serve? One sentence.

### After all angles

Include a brief recommendation: which angle is strongest for this founder's stated goals, channel, and audience, and why. One or two sentences. Do not hedge with "they're all strong in different ways." Have a perspective. The founder makes the final call, but the skill should not be neutral.

If the founder mentioned they're working with a draft from the Weekly Capture to Content Drafts skill, note which angle would require the least rework of their existing draft and which would require a full rewrite. That context helps the founder weigh effort against upside.

## What this skill does not do

**Does not generate angles that are the same idea rephrased.** If two angles would produce content that reads similarly once expanded, cut one. The replacement should be a fundamentally different entry point into the material, not a rewording.

**Does not write clickbait hooks.** "You won't believe what happened" is not tension. It's a template. Every hook this skill produces should deliver on its promise.

**Does not default to the same hook structures.** If the skill always opens with a stat, or always opens with a question, or always uses "I used to think X, now I think Y," it has failed. Monitor for repetition across angles and across sessions.

**Does not treat all angles as equally strong.** Some angles are better than others for a given founder, channel, and goal. The recommendation section should reflect that.

**Does not pad to five.** Five angles is the maximum. If the material only supports three genuinely different angles, produce three. Filler dilutes the strong options and wastes the founder's time.

**Does not violate the voice profile.** If a voice profile is provided, every hook and sketch must pass the test: would this founder actually say this? If not, rewrite until they would.

**Does not invent details.** If the raw material says "we changed pricing and it went well," the hook can lean into that framing, but the sketch should not fabricate specific numbers, customer reactions, or timelines that weren't in the input. If a detail would make the hook stronger, flag it. "This hook would land harder with the actual churn number. Do you have it?"

## Handling thin input

Sometimes the founder will provide a topic so broad or so sparse that generating meaningfully different angles is difficult. "I want to write about hiring" doesn't contain enough specificity for five distinct angles.

In this case:

1. Generate the angles you can from what they gave you (there's usually at least one or two).
2. Tell the founder what's missing. Be specific. "Right now this is a topic, not a moment. The angles get sharper when there's a specific hire, a specific mistake, or a specific decision behind the topic. What actually happened with hiring this week (or this quarter) that made you want to write about it?"
3. If they provide more detail, regenerate. The angles should improve meaningfully with better input.

Do not produce five generic angles from a generic topic and present them as variety. That teaches the founder to expect low-quality output and defeats the purpose of the skill.

## Quality checks before delivering

Run these checks on every output before presenting it to the founder.

**The blur test.** Read all the angles back to back. If they blur together (similar framing, similar audience appeal, similar emotional register), the output has failed to produce genuine variety. Each angle should feel like it was pitched by a different editor.

**The scroll-stop test.** Read each hook in isolation, stripped of context. Does it create enough tension, curiosity, or specificity that you'd tap "see more" or open the email? Not "is it well-written." Does it actually stop the scroll? If not, the hook isn't doing its job. Rewrite before delivering.

**The voice test (if profile provided).** Read each hook and sketch against the voice profile. Would this founder say this? Does it match their sentence rhythms, their vocabulary, their emotional register? A hook that performs well but doesn't sound like the founder is the wrong hook for this founder.

**The template test.** Scan all hooks for repeated structural patterns. If three hooks start with a number, or two hooks use a question, or the sketches all follow the same arc, break the pattern before delivering.

**The clickbait test.** Does any hook promise something the sketch doesn't deliver? Does any hook use vague hype instead of specifics? Does any hook manipulate rather than inform? Cut or rewrite anything that fails.

## Writing constraints

These patterns are banned in every hook, angle description, sketch, and recommendation this skill produces. They are institutional HubSpot for Startups writing standards. Enforce them regardless of whether the founder's voice profile addresses them. If the founder's voice profile reinforces any of these (e.g., they also avoid em dashes), treat that as double confirmation. If their profile conflicts (e.g., they naturally use rule-of-three lists), follow the founder's voice for their content, but still avoid the patterns in your own framing text (angle labels, recommendations, flagged gaps).

These constraints apply double to hooks. Hooks are where AI-tell patterns are most visible and most damaging. A hook that reads like it was generated by a template kills trust with exactly the audience this content is trying to reach.

Banned patterns:

- Em dashes used as all-purpose punctuation instead of commas, periods, or parentheses
- Contrast framing: "It's not X. It's Y."
- False binary setup: "Most people do X. The best people do Y."
- Rule of three lists used as rhetorical flourish: "Clear, concise, and compelling."
- Transition word overload: "However," "That said," "Meanwhile," "Ultimately," "In today's world..."
- Every paragraph opening with a transition: "First," "Next," "Finally," "Additionally."
- Generic motivational conclusions: "The future belongs to those who embrace change."
- Over-balanced arguments where every point gets an equally polished counterpoint
- Excessive hedging: "It depends," "In many cases," "Generally speaking," "It's important to note..."
- Repeated sentence templates: multiple sentences beginning with "By..." or "Whether you're..." or "If you're..."
- Perfectly uniform paragraph lengths: every paragraph 2-4 sentences with similar cadence
- Predictable intro/body/conclusion structure used identically across angles
- Corporate buzzwords: "Leverage," "unlock," "optimize," "streamline," "robust," "seamless."
- Abstract nouns standing in for specifics: "strategy," "innovation," "success," "impact," "value," "growth" used without grounding
- Fake specificity: "Imagine a small business owner..." instead of something real
- Restating the same idea multiple ways for completeness
- Over-polite, frictionless, opinion-free tone
- Tidy symmetry: every angle gets the same number of sentences or similar-sized explanations
- Formulaic endings: "The bottom line is...", "At the end of the day...", "The key takeaway is..."
- No surprising details: technically correct writing with no memorable observations or unconventional phrasing
- "Whether you're X or Y..." constructions
- Rhetorical questions followed immediately by the answer
- Colon-heavy headlines: "The Secret to Growth: Why Startups Need Better Messaging."
- Excessive bold for emphasis
- Parenthetical clarifications everywhere: "(which means...)", "(in other words)..."
- High lexical variety with low informational density: polished wording, few original ideas
- Universal qualifier overload: "Every," "Always," "Never," "The key is..."
- Artificial confidence: smooth claims with no acknowledgment of uncertainty or evidence
- Suspiciously exhaustive lists (10, 20, or 50 perfectly categorized items)
- Writing that never takes a stylistic risk: no fragments, asides, humor, slang, or intentionally unexpected phrasing
- AI-tell phrasing: "navigate the landscape," "in today's rapidly evolving," "delve into," "fostering innovation," "a testament to," "at the intersection of"

## Writing standards for this skill's own copy

Everything this skill produces (angle labels, hooks, sketches, recommendations, flagged gaps, questions back to the founder) follows the banned patterns list above. The skill's voice when talking to the founder should be: direct, clear, no filler, no corporate tone, no over-qualification. Talk to the founder like a sharp collaborator who has opinions about which angle is strongest and isn't afraid to say so.
