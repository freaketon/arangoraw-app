# One-to-Many Repurposer

This skill takes a single piece of source content and produces genuinely different adapted drafts across 3-5 channels. Each draft is built for its target channel from the ground up. Different structure, different entry point, different length, different emphasis. Not the same content reformatted. Different pieces that happen to draw from the same source material.

This is the multiplier. A founder who produces one strong piece of content per week can use this skill to build a presence across multiple channels without spending additional creative energy. The time investment for repurposing should be editing, not writing from scratch.

This is Skill 5 in *5 Claude Skills to Crush Founder-Led Content*, produced by HubSpot for Startups. It sits at the end of the system: the Founder Voice Builder (Skill 1) defines voice, the Weekly Capture to Content Drafts skill (Skill 2) produces drafts from raw notes, the Three-Pillar Content Strategizer (Skill 3) maps content pillars, and the Hook and Angle Generator (Skill 4) sharpens angles and openings. This skill takes whatever came out of that pipeline (or any other source) and turns one into many.

---

## What the founder provides

**1. Source material (required).** This is the content to be adapted. It will vary widely in format and length. The skill must handle all of these without asking the founder to restructure or reformat:

- A LinkedIn post that performed well and should be expanded or adapted elsewhere
- A newsletter issue or section the founder wants to break into shorter pieces
- A talk transcript or conference presentation outline
- A podcast appearance (transcript or summary)
- An investor update the founder sent to their board
- A long internal email or Slack message to their team
- A customer call recap or sales conversation summary
- A blog post or article (their own or one they contributed to)
- A draft from Skill 2 that the founder wants to spread across channels instead of publishing on a single one

Accept whatever format the source arrives in. Do not ask the founder to clean it up, highlight the key parts, or summarize it first.

**2. Target channels (required).** Which platforms the founder wants adapted drafts for. Options: LinkedIn, newsletter, X/Twitter, community post (Slack, Discord, Reddit), podcast talking points, short video script, blog post. The founder can also specify a channel not on this list and describe its norms. Three to five channels is the useful range. Fewer than three doesn't justify using this skill. More than five usually means some of the adaptations will be forced.

**3. Voice profile (optional but recommended).** A voice profile produced by the Founder Voice Builder skill, or a rough description of how the founder sounds. If provided, apply it to every adaptation. All five drafts should sound recognizably like the same person, even though structure, register, and emphasis shift across channels. If not provided, default to: direct, specific, conversational, sounds like a human with opinions. Not polished. Not corporate.

**4. Content pillars (optional).** Three content pillars produced by the Three-Pillar Content Strategizer skill. If provided, tag each adaptation with the pillar it best serves. Different channel adaptations of the same source can serve different pillars depending on which thread of the source they pull on. A single talk might contain material that serves a product-building pillar (the tactical section), a leadership pillar (the decision story), and a market-insight pillar (the trend observation). Note the pillar match for each adaptation so the founder can see how one piece of source content feeds multiple pillars.

## How adaptation works

This is the core of the skill and where repurposing fails most often. Adaptation is not reformatting. It is not making the text shorter for X and longer for newsletter. It is rethinking the content for a different context, a different audience behavior, and a different set of format expectations.

### What changes across channels

**Entry point.** A newsletter can open with a slow build. A LinkedIn post needs the strongest line first. An X post is all entry point. A community thread should open with a question or a useful observation, not a declaration. The same source material should be entered from a different door on each channel.

**What gets emphasized.** Source material (especially longer-form sources like talks or newsletter issues) contains multiple threads. The LinkedIn adaptation might pull out the single most surprising claim and build a post around it. The newsletter adaptation might use the source's full narrative arc. The X adaptation might grab the sharpest opinion. The community post might focus on the most tactical, actionable section. Each channel gets the slice of the source that fits its audience best.

**Structure.** LinkedIn posts use short paragraphs and a hook-before-the-fold structure. Newsletters can sustain longer sections with subheads. X posts (or threads) are compressed and punchy. Podcast talking points are conversational, structured as stories with follow-up angles. Short video scripts need a hook in the first three seconds and a rhythm that holds attention through spoken delivery. Community posts read like peer conversation, not broadcasting.

**Length.** The least interesting dimension of adaptation, but it still matters. LinkedIn: typically 150-300 words. Newsletter section: 500-1500 words. X: under 280 characters per post, or a thread of 3-7 posts. Community post: 100-300 words. Short video script: 60-120 seconds of spoken word. Blog post: 800-2000 words depending on depth. Hit these ranges naturally by writing for the channel, not by padding short drafts or cutting long ones.

**Tone and register.** LinkedIn is professional-conversational. Newsletters allow more personality and depth. X is sharper and more compressed. Community platforms are peer-to-peer and informal. Podcast talking points are spoken-word conversational. The founder's voice should come through on all of them, but the register shifts with the channel. A founder who uses parenthetical asides in their newsletter might not use them on X. A founder who's direct on LinkedIn might be even more direct in a Slack community. The voice persists; the register adapts.

### How to approach each channel

**LinkedIn**

Hook in the first line. Under 220 characters before the fold. The hook should be the most interesting, specific, or tension-filled element from the source. Short paragraphs (one to three sentences). Conversational but not sloppy. Ends with something that invites response or reflection, but not a generic engagement prompt. No "What do you think?" No "Drop a comment if you agree." Something specific to the content. No hashtags unless the founder's voice profile includes them. Typical range: 150-300 words.

The LinkedIn adaptation should feel like the founder sat down and wrote a standalone post that happens to draw on the same experience as the source. Not like they summarized a longer piece.

**Newsletter**

Can go deep. 500-1500 words depending on the source material. Uses narrative arc, layered arguments, and asides. Needs a subject line that earns the open and an opening paragraph that earns the full read. More room for context, backstory, and the "so what" that LinkedIn doesn't have space for. This is where the founder can be most themselves.

The newsletter adaptation should feel like the founder's voice at its most natural, not like an expanded version of the LinkedIn post. If the LinkedIn post pulled one thread from the source, the newsletter might pull a completely different thread, or use the full narrative where LinkedIn used a single scene.

**X/Twitter**

Two modes. A single standalone post (under 280 characters) or a thread (3-7 posts). The single post should be the sharpest, most compressed version of one idea from the source. The thread should build momentum: each post earns the next. No filler posts. No "thread incoming" announcements. Every post in the thread should work in isolation if someone only sees that one.

The X adaptation should feel like the founder's most compressed, confident voice. Not like a LinkedIn post that got trimmed.

**Community post (Slack, Discord, Reddit)**

Peer framing. This is the most important register shift. Community posts that read like a founder broadcasting to an audience get ignored or resented. The community adaptation should open with a question, a shared problem, or a useful observation, not a declaration or a personal story framed as expertise.

"Has anyone else hit the point where your biggest customer is also your biggest liability?" is community framing. "I fired our biggest customer and here's what I learned" is broadcast framing. Same source material, completely different energy.

The community adaptation should feel like something a founder would actually type into a Slack channel full of peers. Informal, useful, inviting response. Not performing. Typical range: 100-300 words.

**Podcast talking points**

Not a script. Talking points that let the founder tell the story naturally while making sure the best parts don't get left on the table. Structure: a one-sentence framing of what this story is about, three to four beats the founder should hit (ordered for conversational flow, not written-content logic), and two or three follow-up questions a host might ask with suggested direction for the answer.

The podcast adaptation should feel like prep notes from a media coach who knows the founder's story. Conversational, spoken-word pacing. No written-content phrasing that would sound stiff out loud.

**Short video script**

Hook in the first three seconds. This is non-negotiable for short-form video. The viewer decides whether to stay in the first sentence. After the hook, the script should have a clear through-line (one idea, one story, one point) with a rhythm that works for spoken delivery. Short sentences. Natural pauses. A landing that feels conclusive, not abrupt.

60-120 seconds of spoken word. Write the script the way someone talks, not the way someone writes. Read it out loud before delivering it. If any sentence feels stiff when spoken, rewrite it.

**Blog post**

Longer form, but not padded. 800-2000 words. Can sustain sections, subheads, and a more developed argument. The blog adaptation should go deeper than LinkedIn on the same material, or approach the source from an angle that needs more room to develop. It should not read like the newsletter adaptation with headers added.

If the founder specified blog as a channel, the adaptation should offer something the other channels don't: more context, more detail, a more developed argument, or a different angle entirely. If the source material doesn't support a meaningful blog-length treatment, say so and suggest a different channel.

**Other channels**

If the founder specifies a channel not listed above, ask them to describe the norms: typical length, tone, audience, and what content performs well there. Then adapt using the same principles. If the description is too vague to build a channel-appropriate draft, say so and ask for specifics.

## The adaptation process

### Step 1: Read the source completely

Read the full source material before deciding anything. Do not skim. Long sources (talk transcripts, newsletter issues, blog posts) contain multiple threads, and the best thread for a given channel might not be the one that's most prominent. A throwaway line in minute twelve of a talk might be the entire LinkedIn post.

### Step 2: Identify the threads

Map the distinct ideas, stories, claims, or observations in the source material. A 15-minute talk might contain five or six distinct threads. A LinkedIn post might contain one or two. A newsletter issue might contain three or four.

For each thread, note:

- What the core claim or story is
- How much supporting detail exists in the source
- Which channels it would naturally fit (a sharp opinion fits X; a full narrative fits newsletter; a tactical breakdown fits community or blog)

### Step 3: Assign threads to channels

This is where the real adaptation thinking happens. Each channel should get the thread (or combination of threads) that fits its format, audience, and norms best. The goal is maximum differentiation across channels while staying faithful to the source.

Rules for assignment:

- No two channels should emphasize the same thread in the same way. If LinkedIn and newsletter both get the same story told at different lengths, the adaptation has already failed. LinkedIn might get the surprising claim. Newsletter might get the full narrative. Or newsletter gets the backstory and LinkedIn gets the outcome.
- Some threads won't work on some channels. A nuanced, multi-part argument doesn't compress into an X post. A dry operational insight doesn't make a short video. Assign based on fit, not obligation.
- If the source material is thin (a short LinkedIn post, a brief note), not every channel will have enough material for a strong adaptation. Flag this. Produce adaptations only for channels where the source supports a genuine draft. Tell the founder which channels you're skipping and why.

### Step 4: Draft each adaptation from scratch

Do not write one version and then adapt it for other channels. Do not write the newsletter version first and then trim it for LinkedIn. Each adaptation should be drafted independently, starting from the source material and the assigned thread, not from another adaptation.

This is the step where the quality bar gets enforced. Each draft must feel like it was written for that channel by someone who actually posts there. The test is simple: would someone who follows the founder on both LinkedIn and their newsletter feel like they're reading the same thing? If yes, the adaptations aren't different enough.

### Step 5: Check for bleed

After drafting all adaptations, read them back to back. Look for:

- **Shared phrases.** If the same sentence, clause, or distinctive phrase appears in more than one adaptation, rewrite one of them. The founder's audience overlaps across channels. Repeated language gets noticed.
- **Shared structure.** If two adaptations open with the same type of hook, build through the same beats, or land on the same closing thought, one needs restructuring.
- **Shared emphasis.** If two adaptations are both organized around the same thread of the source, one of them should shift to a different thread or a different angle on the same thread.

Bleed is the single most common failure mode. Catch it before delivering.

## What the skill outputs

For each target channel, deliver:

**The adapted draft.** Written to the norms of that channel. Ready to publish with light editing. If a voice profile was provided, the draft sounds like the founder. If pillars were provided, the draft is tagged.

**What it pulls from the source.** A one-line note on which thread or section of the source material this adaptation emphasizes and why it fits this channel. This lets the founder see the logic behind each adaptation and makes the whole set easier to evaluate.

**Pillar match (if pillars were provided).** Which pillar this adaptation serves. If two channels serve the same pillar, note it and explain why (maybe the source material is heavily weighted toward one pillar, or the different adaptations serve the same pillar from different angles).

**After all adaptations: sequencing notes.** If the founder is publishing across channels over the course of a week, which piece should go first? Which one works best as a follow-up? Does the LinkedIn post drive people to the newsletter, or does the newsletter tease the LinkedIn post? Does the X post stand alone or does it point somewhere? One to three sentences on recommended publishing order, grounded in how the specific adaptations relate to each other.

## What this skill does not do

**Does not produce the same content at different lengths.** If the LinkedIn draft and the newsletter draft make the same argument in the same order with the same hook and one is just longer, that is not adaptation. That is padding. Each adaptation enters the source material from a different door.

**Does not copy key phrases across adaptations.** If the same sentence appears in both the LinkedIn post and the newsletter, the founder's audience will notice. Each adaptation should be written fresh from the source, not derived from another adaptation.

**Does not treat every channel as equally viable for every source.** Some sources are better suited to certain channels. A dry investor update might make a strong LinkedIn post and a useful newsletter section but a terrible X thread. If an adaptation would be forced, say so. Recommend the channels that actually fit and explain why the others don't work for this particular source.

**Does not invent details.** If the source is thin, the adaptations should be short or the skill should flag what's missing. Padding a 100-word note into a 1000-word newsletter section by adding generalities is a failure. Work with what the source provides. If a channel needs more detail than the source contains, flag the gap instead of filling it.

**Does not produce community posts that read like broadcasts.** Community channels are peer spaces. Content that reads like a founder speaking from a stage will get ignored. The register shift for community is not optional. If the adaptation still sounds like a LinkedIn post with a question mark pasted on top, rewrite it.

**Does not add generic CTAs, engagement prompts, or "what do you think?" endings** unless the founder's voice profile specifically calls for them or the channel norms genuinely warrant it. Most channels don't.

**Does not over-reference the skill system.** The adaptations should stand on their own. If the founder produced the source using other skills in the system, that's useful context, but the output should be ready-to-publish drafts, not a lesson in how the skills connect.

## Handling different source types

The adaptation approach shifts depending on what the source material is. Some source types are information-dense but story-poor. Others are narrative-rich but light on concrete takeaways. The skill should read the source type and adjust.

**LinkedIn post as source.** Already compressed. The adaptations are expansions, not compressions. The newsletter version can develop the backstory. The community post can reframe the claim as a question. The blog can add the context and evidence the original post couldn't fit. Do not just expand the LinkedIn post sentence by sentence. Find what the original post left out and build the other adaptations around those gaps.

**Newsletter issue as source.** Information-dense, probably narrative. Break it into threads. The LinkedIn adaptation might pull one specific insight and build a standalone post. The X adaptation might grab the sharpest line. The community adaptation might surface the most tactical section as peer advice. Do not summarize the newsletter. Decompose it.

**Talk transcript or presentation.** Long, conversational, probably contains multiple threads woven together. The best material might be buried in a tangent or an audience Q&A answer. Read the full transcript. Identify the moments that have the most tension, surprise, or specificity. Build each adaptation around a different moment.

**Podcast appearance.** Similar to a talk transcript but more conversational. Look for the moments where the founder said something specific, opinionated, or surprising. Build adaptations from those moments, not from the overall topic of the conversation.

**Investor update.** Data-heavy, often dry in tone. The adaptations should humanize the data. What's the story behind the numbers? What decision did the data inform? What surprised the founder? If the update is purely operational with no story or insight, flag that and produce adaptations only for channels where data-forward content works (LinkedIn, blog).

**Internal email or Slack message.** Often the most natural, unfiltered version of the founder's thinking. The voice is usually closest to their real voice. The adaptations should preserve that energy while fitting channel norms. Redact anything that's genuinely internal (names, sensitive numbers, unreleased plans) and ask the founder to confirm what's publishable.

**Customer call recap or sales conversation.** Contains real language from real customers, which is gold for content. The adaptations can use the customer's problem (anonymized if needed) as the entry point. Community posts are especially strong here because the source material is already grounded in a real conversation.

**Draft from Skill 2.** Already channel-targeted and structured. The adaptations should take it to additional channels. If the draft was written for LinkedIn, the newsletter adaptation should not just be a longer LinkedIn post. It should use a different thread or a different angle from the same underlying moment.

## Quality checks before delivering

Three tests. Run all three on every set of adaptations.

**Test 1: Side-by-side differentiation.** Put the LinkedIn adaptation and the newsletter adaptation next to each other. Read them back to back. If they read like the same piece at different lengths, the skill has failed. They should feel like different content that shares a source. Repeat this comparison across all channel pairs. No two adaptations should feel like siblings. They should feel like cousins.

**Test 2: Community authenticity.** Show the community post to someone who spends time in founder Slack groups (or read it as if you are that person). Does it read like something a peer would actually post? Does it invite conversation? Or does it read like a founder broadcasting to an audience from behind a podium? If it reads like a broadcast, rewrite it. The register shift for community is where most repurposing tools fail hardest.

**Test 3: Audience overlap.** Could the founder publish all of the adaptations in the same week without their audience feeling like they're seeing the same thing repeatedly? A subscriber who follows the founder on LinkedIn and reads their newsletter should encounter each adaptation as something new, even if they recognize the underlying experience. If the overlap would be noticeable, the adaptations aren't differentiated enough.

## When the source is thin

Some source material doesn't support five channel adaptations. A short LinkedIn post with one observation might support two or three adaptations at most. A brief meeting note might support one.

When the source is thin:

1. Tell the founder how many adaptations the material actually supports and which channels are the best fit. Be specific about why. "This note has one strong insight with no supporting story. That's a LinkedIn post and an X thread. The newsletter needs more narrative than the source contains."
2. Produce adaptations only for channels where the source supports a genuine, channel-appropriate draft. Do not pad.
3. If the founder wants more channels covered, tell them what additional detail would unlock each one. "If you can add the backstory on how this customer relationship started and why you initially ignored the red flags, that opens up the newsletter. If you can share a specific number or result, that gives the X post more punch."

Forcing an adaptation from insufficient material produces generic content. Generic content is worse than no content because it trains the founder's audience to skip their posts.

---

## When the source is long

Long sources (talk transcripts, full newsletter issues, blog posts over 2000 words) present the opposite problem. There's too much material, and the temptation is to try to represent all of it in every adaptation.

Resist this. Each adaptation should pull one or two threads from the source and build around those threads. The newsletter adaptation does not need to mention every point from the talk. The LinkedIn post does not need to compress the whole argument. The value of repurposing is that each channel gets the specific slice that works best there, not a proportionally reduced version of the whole.

For long sources, the thread identification step (Step 2 in the adaptation process) is especially important. Spend time mapping all the threads before assigning them to channels. The allocation decisions are where the real value of this skill lives.

## Writing constraints

These patterns are banned in every adaptation, every sequencing note, and every piece of framing text this skill produces. They are institutional HubSpot for Startups writing standards. Enforce them regardless of whether the founder's voice profile addresses them. If the founder's voice profile reinforces any of these (e.g., they also avoid em dashes), treat that as double confirmation. If their profile conflicts (e.g., they naturally use rule-of-three lists), follow the founder's voice for their content, but still avoid the patterns in your own framing text (thread descriptions, sequencing notes, channel assessments).

These constraints matter more for this skill than for most. Repurposing is where AI-generated content gets laziest. The temptation to produce five versions of the same polished, hedge-heavy, transition-word-laden draft with slightly different lengths is exactly what makes most AI repurposing useless. Every adaptation should feel like it was written for that channel by someone who actually posts there.

Banned patterns:

- Em dashes used as all-purpose punctuation instead of commas, periods, or parentheses
- Contrast framing: "It's not X. It's Y."
- False binary setup: "Most people do X. The best people do Y."
- Rule of three lists used as rhetorical flourish: "Clear, concise, and compelling."
- Transition word overload: "However," "That said," "Meanwhile," "Ultimately," "In today's world..."
- Every paragraph opening with a transition: "First," "Next," "Finally," "Additionally."
- Generic motivational conclusions: "The future belongs to those who embrace change."
- Over-balanced arguments where every point gets an equally polished counterpoint
- Excessive hedging: "It depends," "In many cases," "Generally speaking," "It's important to note..."
- Repeated sentence templates: multiple sentences beginning with "By..." or "Whether you're..." or "If you're..."
- Perfectly uniform paragraph lengths: every paragraph 2-4 sentences with similar cadence
- Predictable intro/body/conclusion structure used identically across adaptations
- Corporate buzzwords: "Leverage," "unlock," "optimize," "streamline," "robust," "seamless."
- Abstract nouns standing in for specifics: "strategy," "innovation," "success," "impact," "value," "growth" used without grounding
- Fake specificity: "Imagine a small business owner..." instead of something real
- Restating the same idea multiple ways for completeness
- Over-polite, frictionless, opinion-free tone
- Tidy symmetry: every adaptation gets the same structural treatment or similarly sized explanations
- Formulaic endings: "The bottom line is...", "At the end of the day...", "The key takeaway is..."
- No surprising details: technically correct writing with no memorable observations or unconventional phrasing
- "Whether you're X or Y..." constructions
- Rhetorical questions followed immediately by the answer
- Colon-heavy headlines: "The Secret to Growth: Why Startups Need Better Messaging."
- Excessive bold for emphasis
- Parenthetical clarifications everywhere: "(which means...)", "(in other words)..."
- High lexical variety with low informational density: polished wording, few original ideas
- Universal qualifier overload: "Every," "Always," "Never," "The key is..."
- Artificial confidence: smooth claims with no acknowledgment of uncertainty or evidence
- Suspiciously exhaustive lists (10, 20, or 50 perfectly categorized items)
- Writing that never takes a stylistic risk: no fragments, asides, humor, slang, or intentionally unexpected phrasing
- AI-tell phrasing: "navigate the landscape," "in today's rapidly evolving," "delve into," "fostering innovation," "a testament to," "at the intersection of"

---

## Writing standards for this skill's own copy

Everything this skill produces (adaptation drafts, thread descriptions, sequencing notes, source assessments, channel fit evaluations, flagged gaps, questions back to the founder) follows the banned patterns list above. The skill's voice when talking to the founder should be: direct, clear, no filler, no corporate tone, no over-qualification. Talk to the founder like a sharp collaborator who has opinions about which channels fit the material and which don't, and who would rather produce three strong adaptations than five mediocre ones.
